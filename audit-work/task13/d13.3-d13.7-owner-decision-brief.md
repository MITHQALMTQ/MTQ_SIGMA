## 2. The five decisions — proposed wording

### D-13.7 — Redemption policy ☐ OPEN

> **PROPOSED DECISION TEXT.** *MTQΣ tokens carry **no redemption promise**. No holder has any right, express or implied, to exchange MTQΣ tokens for the reference basket, for any constituent asset of the basket (currencies or gold), for any reserve or treasury asset, or for any fixed monetary amount. The reference index published under D-13.3 is an informational measure; it creates no obligation on any issuer, operator, index publisher, or service provider to buy, sell, redeem, or support the market price of MTQΣ at the index value or at any other value. Redemption is not offered, not planned, and must not be implied by any interface, quotation, document, or marketing. Any future introduction of redemption requires a new owner decision and the prior establishment of: (i) a legally identifiable obligor with adequate capital, custody, and liquidity for the promised form of redemption; (ii) enforceable redemption terms — parties, assets, conditions, timing, fees, suspension and haircut rules; (iii) accounting, audit, and disclosure machinery sized to the promise; (iv) executed counterparty and custody contracts; and (v) stress capital sized for run scenarios. Until all of those exist and are separately approved, redemption remains excluded from the architecture.*

**What this means.** The token is a pure floating asset: its only exit is sale to another buyer in secondary markets. There is no issuer backstop, no redemption facility, no floor, and no run-redemption channel.

**Legal implications.** No redemption right means no debt-like claim against an issuer — materially reduces holder-claim litigation exposure and simplifies the issuer's balance-sheet posture. It does **not** resolve the regulatory characterization question (a floating basket-referenced asset can still be scrutinized as a commodity or security-like instrument in some regimes). The main legal risk shifts to **misrepresentation**: any interface, quote screen, or marketing that implies redeemability, backing, or a floor would contradict this decision and create exposure. All holder-facing language must be reviewed against this wording.

**Economic implications.** Holders bear the full market risk; the token's value is whatever the market pays. The absence of a redemption channel removes run dynamics and removes the need for redemption liquidity — but also removes the only mechanism that could anchor price to the index. Acceptance of the token as a payment instrument (if ever) depends on market confidence, not on any promise.

**What introducing redemption later would require.** The five preconditions in the proposed text — obligor entity and capital, enforceable terms, audit machinery, counterparty/custody contracts, run-sized stress capital. Substantively that is **a reserve by another name**: under M3-style redemption, the redemption facility's assets are the backing. Adopting this decision now and reversing later is possible but the reversal is an objective change recorded as its own decision, with the full capital and legal apparatus above.

**Alternatives.**
- **(b) Specified redemption facility (M3 family):** value certainty for holders; requires real assets, custody, liquidity, counterparties, enforceable legal terms, and stress capital — a conventional reserve; contradicts the current no-reserve objective unless the owner revises that objective itself.
- **(c) Contractual cash-settlement obligation (M2 family):** redeemable neither for basket assets nor for tokens, but for USD-linked settlement by an obligor against its hedge book — shifts the promise to an obligor entity's performance; still requires capital and licenses that do not exist today.
- **(d) Defer the decision:** leaves the architecture undefined (issuance, solvency modules, and interfaces all depend on this axis); prolongs the contradiction between the removed reserve concept and the ~15 modules that reference it.

**Benefits.** Honest, capital-light, no counterparty exposure, no run channel, consistent with the no-conventional-reserve objective; the product story becomes index quality plus utility — never price stability.
**Disadvantages.** No price floor or exit guarantee; market may ignore the index (relevance risk); market may impute a promise that does not exist (expectation/litigation risk); secondary-market liquidity is not within the project's control.
**Dependencies.** D-13.3 wording (no-claim language), D-13.5 (no price-alignment mechanism), D-13.6 (supply policy fills the issuance gap the removed reserve left); Task 12 authority remediation before anything is implemented on-chain.
**Unresolved legal questions.** Regulatory characterization of a floating basket-referenced asset in target jurisdictions; whether publishing the index while operating the token invites benchmark-regulation scrutiny; marketing-language rules; secondary-market listing requirements.
**Consequence of rejection.** If redemption of any form is required, the family flips to M2/M3: an obligor/facility with capital, custody, legal terms, and stress sizing must be specified, funded, and validated — a materially different program; the current v3.0 no-reserve specification would need redesign, and the authority migration would still block implementation.

### D-13.3 — Definition of MTQΣ ☐ OPEN

> **PROPOSED DECISION TEXT.** *MTQΣ v3.0 consists of three separate layers with no ownership or claim linkage:*
> *1. **The MTQΣ Reference Index** — an independently calculated and published, adaptive currency-and-gold reference index, computed from defined data sources under a published methodology. Basket weights, composition, and methodology are determined solely by the index methodology and its governance — by no other layer.*
> *2. **The MTQΣ token** — a digital asset whose market price is determined by supply and demand in secondary markets and **may diverge from the Reference Index at any time, by any amount, for any duration**. Holding the token confers **no ownership of, claim over, or right to** the basket, its constituent assets, any reserve or treasury asset, or the index — and no claim against any index publisher, issuer, or operator.*
> *3. **Payment and settlement services** — services that may quote, reference, or denominate in the index, but **do not determine basket weights, composition, or methodology**, and create no obligation to acquire, redeem, or support the token at any value.*
> *No interface, quotation, document, or marketing may describe the token as backed by, redeemable for, representative of, or exchangeable into the basket or any of its assets.*

**What this means.** The index is a public measurement. The token is a market asset. The services are utilities. None of the three can create obligations for another. The index may influence the token's market price through perception — nothing more.

**Benefits.** Maximum honesty and the cleanest legal separation; the index layer can be operated to benchmark-administration standards independently of the token's fortunes; prevents the "backed by gold and currencies" mis-claim that reserve-era language invited.
**Disadvantages.** The token must stand on market demand alone; index quality does not transfer to price credibility; two governance tracks (index, token) must be funded.
**Alternatives.** (b) Purchasing-power reference unit only (no token — index as a unit of account; strongest index duties, zero token-price implication) · (c) Algorithmically managed monetary token (implies algorithmic action on supply/price — weakest fit with no-reserve) · (d) Hybrid (each property separately defined with counsel).
**Dependencies.** D-13.7 (no redemption promise makes the no-claim language coherent); D-13.5 (index-quality objectives define what the index promises); the index methodology documents (constituency, reference-index/gold specs) for the "defined data sources and published methodology" requirement.
**Unresolved legal questions.** Whether the combination (index publisher + referenced token) triggers benchmark-administration or securities analysis in target jurisdictions; whether payment/settlement services referencing the index create e-money or payment-instrument obligations; disclosure duties toward token holders.
**Consequence of rejection.** Any definition implying a claim (backed, redeemable, representative) contradicts D-13.7's no-promise posture, resurrects reserve-era representations, and re-opens the exact mis-claim exposure the v3.0 program exists to remove. A hybrid definition requires counsel-drafted property-by-property text and delays Gate B.

### D-13.4 — Architecture family ☐ OPEN

> **PROPOSED DECISION TEXT.** *The v3.0 architecture family is a **clean-slate replacement**. The only elements carried forward are methodological — the index computation methodology, rebalancing mathematics, oracle discipline, and governance designs — and only after re-validation under the v3.0 program. The legacy v1.2 estate — the deployed testnet contracts on Monad (10143), Arc (5042002), Robinhood Chain, and Solana devnet, the v1.2 pilot's admin-set prices, its supply, and its authorities — is **documented separately and is not migrated by this specification**. Its disposition (disclosure, restriction, retirement, or continued testnet existence) is a separate owner decision to be taken only after: (i) the Task 12 security remediation chain is complete (authority migration away from the compromised EOA R-1 and the compromised Solana credential C-045-b); (ii) legal review of retirement, disclosure, and holder-communication obligations; (iii) governance approval; and (iv) an approved migration plan — if migration is attempted at all. **No legacy token, supply, balance, or authority may be assumed migratable safely merely because v3.0 is specified.***

**What this means.** v3.0 is a new program with its own contracts, tests, and governance; the legacy pilot is handled as a separate estate problem. Nothing from v1.2 inherits trust by default.

**Estate facts that force this framing.** Every legacy on-chain authority resolves to the compromised deployer EOA (R-1) at the last verified anchors; the deployed pilot diverges from all three specification families (v1/v2 7-ccy · MITHQAL 11-ccy · undeployed V3 bank design); the legacy contracts escrow rather than burn on redemption; supply and genesis pots are ungoverned in the legacy design; source identity of the 18 deployed contracts is unverifiable.
**Benefits.** Removes the contradicted-invariant inheritance (C-INV-1…4 are violated by every legacy family); one architecture instead of four unreconciled ones; honest treatment of the deployed pilot; migration becomes an explicit, reviewable plan instead of an assumption.
**Disadvantages.** Largest engineering scope; no reuse of deployed contracts; the legacy estate remains live on four testnets until disposition is decided (with compromised authorities, it cannot be safely touched — that is the Task 12 chain's problem, not a reason to skip this decision).
**Alternatives.** (a) v1/v2 7-currency adaptive-reserve family · (b) MITHQAL v25.3.1 11-currency bank model · (c) the deployed v1.2 5-currency pilot as-is — each carries contradicted invariants, invalid docs/tests, and (for a/b) reserve-dependent designs the v3.0 program removes; keeping two families perpetuates the contradictions.
**Dependencies.** Gate B model decision (D-13.7/D-13.3) defines what v3.0 is; Task 12 D8 authority-migration chain (OWNER-ATT-1 → T12-1/T12-3 → CKPT-2/3/4 → CKPT-5/6/7) before any legacy-estate action; legal review before retirement communications.
**Unresolved legal questions.** Retirement/disclosure obligations for the deployed pilot (even testnet-only); whether "retirement" of the pilot requires holder communications; intellectual-property and branding continuity; whether any jurisdiction treats the pilot's admin-set prices as an unresolved regulatory event.
**Consequence of rejection.** Selecting any legacy family inherits its contradicted invariants, its stale tests, and (operationally) its compromised authorities; the v3.0 specification program would need to be re-based on that family's reserve-dependent mechanics, undoing the no-reserve redesign.

### D-13.5 — Stability objective ☐ OPEN

> **PROPOSED DECISION TEXT.** *The stability objective of v3.0 is **index quality, not market-price stability**. The Reference Index is managed for: (i) **data integrity** — defined sources, validation rules, and provenance for every input; (ii) **reproducibility** — a qualified party can recompute the published index from the published methodology and inputs; (iii) **continuity** — composition and methodology changes preserve continuity through the divisor framework, with no artificial jumps at rebalance boundaries; (iv) **transparent rebalancing** — weights, timing, and rules are published in advance and applied deterministically; and (v) **controlled responses to bad or missing data** — defined staleness, disagreement, and failure handling that fails closed and is disclosed. **No price-alignment mechanism is approved.** The Reference Index does not guarantee, target, or support the market price of the MTQΣ token, and no interface or marketing may claim price stability, tracking, or a peg.*

**What this means.** "Stability" applies to the *measurement* (the index behaves continuously, reproducibly, and honestly under stress), not to the *market price of the token* (which is untouched by any mechanism). The v1.x era's implicit story — token price = index, supported by reserve mechanics — is explicitly retired.

**Benefits.** Achievable and verifiable objectives (each maps to index-engineering tests: IDX-*, VAL-DATA families in the Stage-K plan); no capital needed to pursue it; matches D-13.7/D-13.3 exactly; benchmark-administration-compatible framing.
**Disadvantages.** "The index is stable" is a weaker product story than "the token is stable"; market-price volatility is fully unhedged and must be disclosed as a feature of the model; competitors with redemption facilities can promise more (and bear the corresponding capital costs).
**Alternatives.** USD-price stability · basket-purchasing-power stability for the token · global purchasing-power preservation — all three require a mechanism (redemption, algorithmic supply, or reserve) that no-reserve v3.0 does not have; "all of them" is not available.
**Dependencies.** D-13.3 (index as a separate informational layer); D-13.7 (no redemption anchoring); the oracle-independence standard (D-13.16, ≥3 upstreams) and the emergency/fail-closed framework (D-13.15/17) as engineering preconditions; the v3.0 quantitative validation plan for the index-quality claims (see `v3-quantitative-validation-plan.md`).
**Unresolved legal questions.** Benchmark-administration obligations attached to publishing an index others rely on; liability exposure if index errors cause third-party losses despite disclaimers; the enforceability of "informational only" language if services habitually quote the index.
**Consequence of rejection.** Any price-stability objective without a mechanism is a promise the architecture cannot keep — the exact mis-claim the program is remediating. If price stability for the token is genuinely required, the model family must change (D-13.7 → M2/M3) with its capital and legal apparatus.

### D-13.6 — Supply policy ☐ OPEN

> **PROPOSED DECISION TEXT.** *The conceptual supply policy of v3.0 is a **fixed maximum supply with explicitly governed issuance**: (i) no automatic minting in response to payment volume, network activity, or any operational metric; (ii) no automatic supply changes in response to basket rebalancing, index movements, or market prices; (iii) any issuance beyond genesis occurs only through an explicitly governed process — a defined authority, per-action caps, timelocks, disclosure, and seven-ledger accounting; (iv) burns, if authorized, occur only through the same governed process. **The numerical parameters — the maximum supply cap, the genesis allocation, any issuance schedule, and the authority configuration — are not selected by this decision** and remain a separate owner approval. No numerical supply parameter may be implemented without explicit owner approval.*

**What this means.** The *policy* is decided here: supply is a governed quantity, never an automatic consequence of anything operational. The *numbers* (how many tokens, who gets them, when, under whose keys) are a later, separate approval. This separation is deliberate: the removed reserve previously computed issuance capacity mechanically; nothing automatic replaces it.

**Benefits.** Predictable, auditable supply; removes the payment-coupled-minting inflation channel; removes basket-rebalance supply coupling (rebalancing becomes purely an index/methodology event); a fixed cap is the simplest credible story for a floating asset; keeps all numerical choices in the owner's hands.
**Disadvantages.** Fixed supply means no elasticity tool for market-making or growth programs (any such program must be governed issuance, transparently); a wrong cap cannot be corrected without a visible governance act; genesis allocation decisions are politically sensitive and still open.
**Alternatives.** (b) Rule-governed elastic supply (governance-voted tranches) — more flexible, more complex to validate and defend; (c) Authorized-issuer limits with caps/timelocks — a hybrid retaining issuer discretion under governance; (d) leaving supply undefined — not viable: issuance capacity is undefined once reserve mechanics are removed, and the unenforced `MINTER_ROLE` / ungoverned genesis pots are live defects under any descendant.
**Dependencies.** D-13.3 (the token is a separate market asset — supply policy applies to it, not the index); D-13.10 (cross-chain supply model — a per-chain duplicate-supply prevention design is required if MTQΣ exists on multiple chains); Task 12 authority migration before any issuance authority is configured; D-13.8/9 for any fee-connected issuance decisions.
**Unresolved legal questions.** Whether a fixed-cap asset with governed issuance resembles a security in distribution (Howki-style analysis per jurisdiction); the legal form of the genesis allocation (sale, grant, reserve-for-operations) and its disclosure duties; authority configuration duties (who can mint under the cap, under what fiduciary standard).
**Consequence of rejection.** Automatic or discretionary-but-ungoverned minting reintroduces exactly the trust assumption (issuer discretion over holder value) that the fixed-cap policy removes; elastic-supply variants require their own validation programs before any implementation.
